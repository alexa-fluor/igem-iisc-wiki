# Integrated Human Practices

## Is MOSKATEER Responsible and Good for the World?

We didn't treat this as a question to answer once, in a single section near the end of the project. Engineering a self-spreading organism for release into a shared environment means the answer has to be revisited every time the technical design changes — so this page is less a report of activities we completed and more a record of a question we kept returning to, and how the answer kept getting more specific as the project did.

## The Issues We Identified

Before we could ask anyone else what they thought of MOSKATEER, we had to be honest with ourselves about what kinds of issues our project actually raises:

**Ethical.** Silencing *mosGILT* doesn't kill mosquitoes — it suppresses a wild population's reproduction, at scale, using an organism that cannot consent and whose ecological role we don't fully understand. That framing, and the broader tension between human public-health benefit and non-human welfare, is discussed in depth on our [Bioethics page](#).

**Social.** Malaria is not evenly distributed as a research priority relative to its burden — it falls hardest on lower-income, rural communities, including many of the regions MOSKATEER targets. That raised an equity question we couldn't ignore: the people who'd bear whatever risk our organism carries are largely the same people who stand to benefit, and have historically had the least say in how such technologies get built.

**Safety and security.** Because *Asaia* is engineered to spread both horizontally and vertically rather than being self-limiting, any error in the design is far harder to recall than in a self-limiting system. We treat this — along with the dual-use potential of a generalizable RNAi-delivery platform — as a safety and security question, detailed further on our [Safety and Security page](#).

**Sustainability.** MOSKATEER's alignment with the UN Sustainable Development Goals — most directly SDG 3 (malaria elimination, Target 3.3) but also SDG 15 (avoiding the biodiversity harm of broad-spectrum insecticides) and SDG 17 (partnership-driven implementation) — is discussed on our [Sustainability page](#), and shaped how we talk about the project's purpose beyond the lab.

## Who We Talked To, and What We Heard

**Clinicians at Ramaiah Hospital, Bengaluru.** We designed a structured questionnaire for medical professionals treating malaria patients, covering both project-specific questions (how they currently manage cases, what gaps they see in existing prevention tools) and broader questions about malaria outbreak patterns in the region. This was a deliberate choice to ground our technical design in clinical reality rather than only in the literature — clinicians see the failure modes of existing interventions (insecticide resistance, inconsistent bed net use, recurring outbreaks) in a way a paper rarely captures.

**The public, through a community awareness survey.** Alongside the clinician questionnaire, we built a broader community-facing survey covering baseline awareness of malaria, perceptions of its scale (both globally and in India specifically), familiarity with existing prevention methods, and — critically — prior awareness of genetic or biological approaches like paratransgenesis and gene drives, including whether people had even encountered the term before. This gave us a direct read on the gap between what we assume the public knows and what they actually know, which matters enormously for a project whose eventual success depends on public trust, not just technical efficacy.

**Government school students, through NoteBook Drive.** Partnering with NoteBook Drive (NBD), a student-run initiative at IISc supporting government schools around Bengaluru, we brought malaria awareness directly to students in grades 1 through 8 — a group with essentially no prior exposure to synthetic biology. Activity sheets, true/false and matching games, and even non-malaria creative activities like origami and thumb painting gave us a genuinely two-way exchange rather than a one-directional lecture: we learned what a 10-year-old already understands about mosquitoes and disease, which shaped how plainly we now try to explain the project to any non-specialist audience.

**Our own institution, across two directions.** We ran orientation sessions that drew over 100 future iGEMers, walking incoming students through what iGEM is, how a project cycle unfolds, and MOSKATEER as a working example — while explicitly encouraging them to start ideating their own project directions rather than just inheriting ours. In the other direction, we invited juniors and incoming undergraduates, many from outside biology entirely, to write about MOSKATEER-related topics in their own words. The diversity and seriousness of the questions we got back — and the fact that several students went on to independently pursue our dry lab tools further — told us our own explanations of the project were legible to people without a biology background, which we hadn't actually verified until we tried.

## How This Fed Back Into the Project

Documenting these conversations only matters if they changed something, so here's where the feedback loop actually closed:

The choice to frame our community survey around *prior awareness* of genetic approaches, rather than assuming the public would default to fear or skepticism, came directly out of early conversations about how badly gene-drive technologies have sometimes been miscommunicated elsewhere — we wanted data on what people actually think before designing our public-facing messaging, not after.

The equity concerns raised in our Bioethics work — that the communities bearing the eventual risk are the same ones with historically the least input — is a large part of why we treat Human Practices as inseparable from the technical project rather than a parallel track: it's the reason clinician and community engagement started well before any environmental-release conversation was relevant, rather than being deferred until the science was "finished."

Running the school outreach and junior write-up programs also directly informed how we now describe MOSKATEER's mechanism to non-specialists across the whole wiki — several of our plainer-language explanations of RNAi and paratransgenesis exist specifically because a version written only for our peers didn't land with a government-school classroom or a first-year undergraduate outside biology.

## Ethical Dilemmas We Haven't Fully Resolved

We don't think it's honest to present this page as though every question got a clean answer. Two, in particular, remain open: first, what community consent should actually look like in the specific regions MOSKATEER would eventually target — informed consent in this context has to work at a community level rather than an individual one, since no single resident can opt an environment out of a release, and we haven't yet worked through what that process would concretely involve. Second, how to weigh a community's own assessment of acceptable risk against our own technical assessment, if the two were ever to diverge — we've named this tension on our Bioethics page, but naming it is not the same as resolving it, and we don't think it can be resolved from inside a lab-stage project.

## Assessing Social Impact — Positive and Negative

**Potential positive impact:** a field-deployable, low-cost intervention that doesn't require the repeated large-scale infrastructure of spraying or net distribution campaigns; a vector-control approach that avoids the ecological cost of broad-spectrum insecticides; and, through our education and awareness work, a small but real increase in baseline public understanding of both malaria and the kind of science being used to address it.

**Potential negative impact:** the ecological uncertainty of a self-sustaining engineered organism released into a wild population, which we have not yet formally risk-assessed because MOSKATEER has not reached the environmental-release stage; the dual-use potential of a generalizable RNA-delivery platform; and the risk that, if public trust isn't built carefully, a genuinely useful tool could be rejected by the communities it's meant to serve — not because it doesn't work, but because it was never properly explained or consented to.

## Looking Ahead

Everything on this page reflects where MOSKATEER stands at a lab-validation stage, engaging clinicians, the public, students, and our own institution well ahead of any environmental release. The harder version of community engagement — actual consultation with the specific communities MOSKATEER would eventually target, run alongside the RCGM/GEAC regulatory process described on our Safety page — hasn't happened yet, because it isn't yet the right stage to do it meaningfully. We see that as the next chapter of this page, not a gap we're pretending isn't there.
