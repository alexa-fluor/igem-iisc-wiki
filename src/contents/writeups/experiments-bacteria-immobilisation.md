# Experiments

## Experiment 1: Bacterial Immobilisation and Viability in the Sugar Trap Matrix

### Introducing the Question

Our delivery strategy depends on getting live, functional *Asaia bogorensis* into a male mosquito's gut through a sugar-based trap in the field — which meant we couldn't simply assume the bacterium would survive being embedded in a sugar matrix long enough to actually be ingested. Sugar solutions are hyperosmotic environments, and high sugar concentrations are a classic way to stress or dehydrate bacterial cells; before we could trust the sugar trap as a delivery vehicle at all, we needed to know whether the sugar matrix itself was quietly killing the very bacteria it was supposed to be carrying.

This experiment was designed to answer that question directly: does embedding *Asaia* in a solidified sugar matrix kill the bacterial cells outright, or does it instead push them into a metabolically inactive, dormant-like state that they can recover from once conditions improve? The distinction matters a great deal for how we interpret every later result involving the trap — a bacterium that survives the trap but stays dormant is still a viable delivery vehicle once ingested, whereas one that's simply dead in the matrix would mean the entire delivery concept needs to be redesigned before we invest further work in it. More specifically, we set out to ask: do bacteria recovered from the sugar matrix regrow into colonies at a rate comparable to bacteria that were never exposed to the matrix, and does time spent embedded in the matrix change that recovery rate?

### Experimental Design and Rationale

We chose to test this with a colony-forming-unit (CFU) recovery assay rather than a direct viability stain, because our real interest wasn't just whether individual cells still had intact membranes — it was whether the bacteria could resume normal growth and division after exposure, which is the property that actually matters for successful colonization once a mosquito ingests them. Recovering bacteria from the matrix and plating them on nutrient-rich LB agar lets dormant-but-viable cells re-enter active growth and form visible colonies, distinguishing them cleanly from cells that were truly killed by the sugar environment and can no longer divide at all.

The trap matrix itself was prepared to mimic the actual field-deployable sugar trap rather than a simplified lab stand-in: a solution of sugar and tartaric acid was heated until fully dissolved, then allowed to cool and solidify into the same kind of solid sugar matrix the field trap uses, rather than testing the bacteria in a liquid sugar solution alone. Tartaric acid was included because it's part of the actual trap formulation (contributing to the matrix's texture and acting as a mild preservative/acidifying agent), so leaving it out would have tested a matrix we don't actually intend to deploy.

- Sugar (sucrose) —

- Distilled water
- LB agar plates (for recovery and colony counting)
- Sterile inoculation loops or spreaders

### Method

1. **Prepare the sugar trap matrix.** Dissolve sugar and tartaric acid in water at by heating on a hot plate until fully dissolved. Allow the solution to cool to a temperature safe for bacterial addition (well below the point at which heat would kill the culture) before proceeding, then let it solidify into a firm matrix, matching the consistency of the field-deployed trap.

2. **Prepare the bacterial culture.** Grow *Asaia bogorensis* overnight in liquid culture under standard conditions until it reaches a consistent growth phase, so that every trial starts from a comparable starting bacterial density.

3. **Embed the bacteria in the matrix.** Introduce a fixed volume of the overnight culture into the sugar matrix while it is still workable (before full solidification), mixing gently to distribute the bacteria evenly rather than concentrating them at one point in the matrix.

4. **Incubate.** Allow the embedded matrix to sit for a defined period to let any osmotic stress response play out.

5. **Recover and plate.** Sample bacteria back out of the sugar matrix and streak or spread them onto LB agar plates, alongside a control plate streaked directly from the original overnight culture that was never exposed to the sugar matrix.

6. **Incubate the plates overnight** at to allow surviving, metabolically active cells to form visible colonies.

7. **Count colonies** on both the matrix-exposed plates and the unexposed control plates, and compare colony counts between the two to assess relative survival and recovery.

### Controls and Replicates

The unexposed overnight culture, plated directly without ever entering the sugar matrix, served as our baseline control — it tells us what colony counts to expect from a comparable starting culture under normal conditions, against which the matrix-exposed samples could be judged.

### What We Expected

If the sugar matrix were simply lethal to *Asaia*, we would expect colony counts from the matrix-exposed samples to fall dramatically or drop to zero relative to the unexposed control, regardless of incubation time. If instead the bacteria enter a metabolically inactive but viable state, we would expect colony counts closer to (though not necessarily identical to) the control, since dormant cells recovered onto nutrient-rich LB agar should be able to resume growth and form colonies once removed from the osmotic stress of the sugar environment. A time-dependent decline in recovery — colony counts dropping the longer bacteria remained embedded — would point toward a slow, cumulative stress effect rather than an immediate kill, and would shape how long we could reasonably expect a field trap to remain effective before needing to be refreshed.
