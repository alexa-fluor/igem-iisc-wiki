## Experiment 4: Glycerol Stock Preparation

### Introducing the Question

Once we had a colony confirmed by colony PCR and verified by restriction digest to carry the correct plasmid, we faced a practical risk rather than a scientific one: that single confirmed colony existed only as a live culture, and live cultures don't last indefinitely on a bench or even in a fridge. Contamination, a failed incubator, or simply time can quietly destroy weeks of confirmation work, and re-doing transformation and screening from scratch every time we needed that exact construct again would have cost us time we couldn't afford to lose repeatedly over the course of the project. The question here wasn't "does this work" in the way our other experiments were — it was whether we could reliably freeze down and later recover the exact confirmed strain, so that every future experiment using this construct was drawing from the same verified source rather than a fresh, unconfirmed transformation each time.

More specifically, we wanted to establish a storage method that would keep the bacteria viable at -80°C over the long term, and confirm that a small sample thawed from that stock could be revived into a healthy, growing culture on demand — effectively treating the glycerol stock itself as an experiment in reproducibility as much as a housekeeping step.

### Experimental Design and Rationale

We used glycerol as a cryoprotectant because it prevents the ice-crystal formation that would otherwise rupture bacterial cell membranes during freezing, which is the standard reason glycerol stocks are the default long-term storage method for bacterial strains across molecular biology labs. Preparing the stock directly from a culture grown from the colony we had already confirmed by PCR and restriction digest (rather than from an unconfirmed colony) meant the stock inherited all of that prior verification — freezing down an unconfirmed culture would have simply preserved uncertainty rather than resolving it.

We deliberately treated "can this stock be revived" as something to actually check, rather than assumed, since a glycerol stock that fails silently would only be discovered as a problem much later, at exactly the point in the project when we most needed a working culture on short notice.

### Materials and Reagents

- Confirmed overnight culture of the positive colony (from Experiments 2 and 3), grown in selective LB broth
- Sterile 50% (or 80%,) glycerol solution
- Sterile cryovials
- Ice (for keeping glycerol solution and mixed stocks cold during preparation)
- -80°C freezer for long-term storage
- Fresh selective LB agar plates (for the revival check)

### Method

1. **Grow an overnight culture** from the confirmed positive colony in selective LB broth at 37°C with shaking, so the stock is prepared from a culture already known to carry the correct plasmid.
2. **Mix the culture with sterile glycerol** in a cryovial at a ratio that brings the final glycerol concentration to, keeping everything on ice during handling to minimize stress on the cells before freezing.
3. **Mix gently but thoroughly** to ensure the glycerol is evenly distributed through the culture, avoiding vortexing.
4. **Label each cryovial clearly** with the construct name, strain, date, and any relevant colony/screening reference, so the stock's identity is traceable later without relying on memory.
5. **Flash-freeze or transfer directly to -80°C** for long-term storage, following whichever of the two your lab's standard practice uses.
6. **Periodically validate revival** by streaking a small sample from the frozen stock onto a fresh selective LB agar plate and confirming that colonies grow overnight at 37°C, checking that the stock remains viable over time rather than assuming it indefinitely.

### Controls and Replicates

We prepared replicate stocks from the same confirmed culture, so that a single freezer failure, contamination event, or failed thaw wouldn't leave us without a backup of that exact confirmed strain. A revival streak from each new batch of stocks served as the working control — successful colony growth after thawing was treated as confirmation that the freezing process itself hadn't compromised viability.

### What We Expected

We expected a properly prepared glycerol stock to remain viable in long-term storage and, when thawed and streaked, to produce healthy colony growth indistinguishable from a fresh culture of the same strain — confirming that neither the freezing process nor storage had damaged or altered the bacteria. Any failure to recover viable colonies on a revival check would indicate a problem with either the freezing protocol or the storage conditions, and would need to be corrected before relying on that stock for future work.
